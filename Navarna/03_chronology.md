## 0. The bottom line

- **The Purāṇa dates the episodes in its own cosmic time**: kalpas, manvantaras and caturyugas. **It gives no calendar date.**
- **Modern scholarship dates the *text*, not the events.** The Devī-Māhātmya was composed most probably in the **5th–6th century CE** (Pargiter, Coburn). Yokochi argues for the **8th century**; I have that only as reported by Policardi 2024. It is certainly earlier than a Nepalese manuscript dated **998 CE**.
- **Buffalo-slaying goddess images go back to the Kuṣāṇa period** (about 1st–3rd c. CE, Mathura), several centuries before the text. Those images carry no name and no myth.
- **I found no historical, archaeological or astronomical evidence that dates the mythological events themselves.** I also found no published, citable attempt to date them astronomically (§4).

## 1. The Purāṇa's internal chronology  [TEXT]

### 1.1 The frame story: Suratha, the Svārociṣa manvantara, and Sāvarṇi as the future 8th Manu

**GP 1.2–1.4:**
> सावर्णिः सूर्यतनयो यो मनुः कथ्यतेऽष्टमः । निशामय तदुत्पत्तिं विस्तराद्गदतो मम ॥ १.२ ॥ महामायानुभावेन यथा मन्वन्तराधिपः । स बभूव महाभागः सावर्णिस्तनयो रवेः ॥ १.३ ॥ स्वारोचिषेऽन्तरे पूर्वं चैत्रवंशसमुद्भवः । सुरथो नाम राजाभूत्समस्ते क्षितिमण्डले ॥ १.४ ॥

"Hear from me in detail the origin of Sāvarṇi, the son of the Sun, who is called the eighth Manu: how, through the power of Mahāmāyā, that fortunate son of the Sun became lord of a manvantara. Formerly, in the Svārociṣa interval, there was a king named Suratha, of the line of Caitra, [ruling] over the whole earth."

**GP 13.22–23, 13.29:**
> मृतश्च भूयः सम्प्राप्य जन्म देवाद्विवस्वतः ॥ सावर्णिको मनुर्नाम भवान्भुवि भविष्यति ॥ … सूर्याज्जन्म समासाद्य सावर्णिर्भविता मनुः ॥ १३.२९ ॥

"And after death, taking birth again from the god Vivasvat, you will be the Manu named Sāvarṇika on earth. … Taking birth from the Sun, he will be Manu Sāvarṇi."

**The time-frame these verses set up:**

| Layer | When, according to the text |
|---|---|
| Mārkaṇḍeya narrates to his pupil (Krauṣṭuki in the larger Purāṇa) | Time of narration. The text places itself in the current (7th, Vaivasvata) manvantara, since Sāvarṇi (the 8th) is still to come. |
| Medhas (Sumedhā) narrates to Suratha and Samādhi | **Svārociṣa manvantara = the 2nd Manu period** ("स्वारोचिषेऽन्तरे पूर्वम्") |
| Episodes narrated by Medhas: Madhu–Kaiṭabha, Mahiṣa, Śumbha–Niśumbha with Caṇḍa–Muṇḍa and Raktabīja | **Before** Medhas's telling: "purā" (see §1.2) |
| Suratha's reward | Future: he will be born of the Sun as **Sāvarṇi, the 8th Manu** |
| Prophecies of DM 11 | Future, in the **Vaivasvata (7th) manvantara** (see §1.3) |

**[COMMENTARY] The commentators make the manvantara numbers explicit.**

- **Śāntanavī on GP 1.2** (DSS7 Vol. 1, p. 40, PDF 51) lists the fourteen Manus of one day of Brahmā:
  > इह खलु ब्रह्मतल्पे चतुर्दश मनवः क्रमशः क्रमन्ते । तद्यथा । 'स्वायंभुवो मनुः पूर्वं ततः स्वारोचिषो मनुः । औत्तमस्तामसश्चैव रैवतश्चाक्षुषस्तथा । षडेते मनवोऽतीता अथ वैवस्वतो मनुः । सावर्णाः पञ्च रौच्यश्च भौत्यश्चागामिनस्त्वमी' । … ततश्च स्वायंभुवाद्याः षट् सप्तमो वैवस्वतः अष्टमः सावर्णिः नवमो दशमः एकादशो द्वादशश्च सावर्णय एव । त्रयोदशो मनू रौच्यः । चतुर्दशस्तु मनुर्भौत्यः ।

  "Here, in one 'bed' (day) of Brahmā, fourteen Manus come in order. Svāyambhuva first, then Svārociṣa, Auttama, Tāmasa, Raivata and Cākṣuṣa: these six are past. Now Vaivasvata. Five Sāvarṇas, Raucya and Bhautya are to come. … So: six from Svāyambhuva; 7th Vaivasvata; 8th Sāvarṇi; 9th–12th also Sāvarṇis; 13th Raucya; 14th Bhautya."

- **Nāgojībhaṭṭa on GP 1.4** (DSS7 Vol. 1, p. 44, PDF 55):
  > स्वारोचिषाधिकारोपलक्षिते द्वितीयमन्वन्तरे पूर्वमिति कथाकालापेक्षया ।

  "'In the Svārociṣa': in the second manvantara, marked by Svārociṣa's reign. 'Formerly' is relative to the time of the narration."

  The Daṃśoddhāra commentary on the same verse says "स्वारोचिषे द्वितीयमन्वन्तरे", and the Śāntanavī glosses पूर्वम् as "पुराकल्पे".

### 1.2 The episodes themselves: "at the end of a kalpa" and "formerly"

- **Madhu–Kaiṭabha.** GP 1.66–1.67 places this at the **end of a kalpa**, when the world is one ocean and Viṣṇu is in yoganidrā:
  > योगनिद्रां यदा विष्णुर्जगत्येकार्णवीकृते । आस्तीर्य शेषमभजत् कल्पान्ते भगवान् प्रभुः ॥ तदा द्वावसुरौ घोरौ विख्यातौ मधुकैटभौ ॥ १.६७ ॥

  Viṣṇu then fights them for "five thousand years" (GP 1.94: पञ्चवर्षसहस्राणि बाहुप्रहरणो विभुः).
- **Mahiṣāsura.** GP 2.2 gives the war a duration of a full hundred years, "long ago":
  > देवासुरमभूद्युद्धं पूर्णमब्दशतं पुरा ।
- **Śumbha–Niśumbha**, together with the Caṇḍa–Muṇḍa and Raktabīja episodes of Ch. 7–8, which belong to the same war. GP 5.2:
  > पुरा शुम्भनिशुम्भाभ्यामसुराभ्यां शचीपतेः । त्रैलोक्यं यज्ञभागाश्च हृता मदबलाश्रयात् ॥

  "Long ago, Śacī's lord [Indra] was robbed of the three worlds and the sacrificial shares by the asuras Śumbha and Niśumbha."

The text gives **no interval** between these episodes, and **no manvantara or yuga** for Mahiṣa or Śumbha–Niśumbha beyond "purā" relative to Medhas's narration in the Svārociṣa period. The Pāñcarātra **Lakṣmī Tantra** does assign manvantaras: Mahiṣa in Svāyambhuva, Śumbha–Niśumbha in Tāmasa (see §3.1). That placement conflicts with the DM's own frame.

**[COMMENTARY] Śāntanavī supplies an order.** On GP 11.41 (DSS7 Vol. 3, p. 252, PDF 63):
> पूर्वं जगदुपकाराय मया देव्या … मधुकैटभौ विष्णुना मारितौ तदनु स्वल्पान्तरं महिषासुरो मया देव्या निपातितः । तदनु चण्डमुण्डधूम्रलोचनरक्तबीजशुम्भनिशुम्भाः ससैन्याः संग्रामे संनिपातिताः ।

"First Madhu and Kaiṭabha were killed by Viṣṇu [through me]. After a short interval Mahiṣāsura was felled by me. After that Caṇḍa, Muṇḍa, Dhūmralocana, Raktabīja, Śumbha and Niśumbha were struck down in battle with their armies."

The phrase "a short interval" (स्वल्पान्तरम्) is the commentator's, not the text's.

- **Bhāskararāya's conclusion: all six are still future.** Guptavatī on GP 11.52, p. 256, PDF 67:
  > भीमादेव्यवतारस्तु नाद्यापि जातः । वैवस्वत एव मन्वन्तरे पञ्चाशत्तमे चतुर्युगे भविष्यतीति लक्ष्मीतन्त्रादिति केचित् । वस्तुतस्तु रक्तदन्त्यादयः षडप्यवतारा भाविन एव । मूले पुनरपि, भूयश्च, पुनश्चाहं, यदारुणाख्यः, इत्येभिः पदैरुत्तरोत्तरकालस्यैव कीर्तनात् । 'तस्मिन्नेवान्तरे शक्र चत्वारिंशत्तमे युगे' इत्युपक्रम्य शताक्ष्यवतारस्य तन्त्रान्तरे कथनात् । तस्मिन्नेवेत्यस्य वैवस्वत इत्यर्थः । 'चतुर्युगेऽत्र पञ्चाशत्तमे मुनिभिरर्चिता' इत्युपक्रम्य भीमादेवीकथनाच्च । तत्रैव 'युगे षष्टितमे कश्चिदरुणो नाम दानवः' इत्यादिकथनाच्च । परंतु सांप्रतिकाच्छ्वेतवराहकल्पात्प्राक्तनकल्पेष्वपि देव्यवताराणामेतेषां मन्वन्तरयुगभेदेन जातत्वाच्छाकम्भर्यादीनां तत्तत्कुलदेवतात्वेनार्चनमधुनातनानां सङ्गच्छत एव ॥

  Translation:
  - "The Bhīmā-devī avatāra has **not happened yet**. Some say, on the basis of the Lakṣmī Tantra, that it will happen in the 50th caturyuga of this very Vaivasvata manvantara.
  - In fact **all six avatāras beginning with Raktadantī are future**, because the root text uses the words 'again', 'once more', 'and again I', 'when Aruṇa…', which announce successively later times.
  - Another tantra introduces the Śatākṣī avatāra with 'In that same interval, O Śakra, in the fortieth yuga', and 'that same' means Vaivasvata. It introduces Bhīmā-devī with 'in the fiftieth caturyuga here, worshipped by the munis', and says there 'in the sixtieth yuga a dānava named Aruṇa…'.
  - Nevertheless, these avatāras also occurred in kalpas before the present Śveta-varāha kalpa, in their various manvantaras and yugas. So the present-day worship of Śākambharī and the others as family deities is entirely consistent."

  As far as I can see, Bhāskararāya's statement is not echoed by the other commentators on this page.

### 1.4 The arithmetic as the Purāṇa itself gives it  [TEXT]

From the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa's own chapter on time-reckoning, in Pargiter's translation (canto XLVI, pp. 226–227):
- "Now of twelve thousand divine years consist the four ages named the Krita, the Tretā, …". Kṛta = 4,000 divine years plus twilights of 400 + 400; Tretā = 3,000 + 300 + 300; Dvāpara = 2,000 + 200 + 200; Kali = 1,000, and "two hundreds of years are called its commencing and closing twilights" (i.e. 100 + 100, which is what the 12,000 total requires).
- "a thousand times this period are called one of Brahmā's days. In one of Brahmā's days … there may be fourteen Manus."
- v. 34–36: "Seventy-one repetitions of the four ages, with a fraction in excess, constitute a manvantara; … Thirty full crores … and sixty-seven lakhs more … and twenty thousands—this is the period of seventy-one times the four ages without the excess fraction".
- Pargiter's footnote: "one divine year = 360 human years"; "The four ages contain 12,000 divine years or 4,320,000 human years, and 71 times this period contain 8,52,000 divine years, or 306,720,000 human years." He also notes inconsistencies in the transmitted text (v. 37 and v. 38).

Applying these numbers to the DM's own markers is simple arithmetic on the texts' figures. **It is not evidence of anything historical.**

- **Svārociṣa (2nd manvantara) to the 28th caturyuga of Vaivasvata (7th).** The texts specify no position *within* Svārociṣa. So the interval is "the remainder of the 2nd manvantara, plus manvantaras 3–6 in full (4 × 71 caturyugas), plus 27 complete caturyugas of the 7th, plus part of the 28th". On the figures above that is at least 4 × 306,720,000 + 27 × 4,320,000 = **1,343,520,000 human years**. The bound is approximate because it ignores the sandhi "excess fraction" Pargiter mentions.
- **Where the 28th caturyuga falls in the commentators' reading.** All the commentators put it at the Dvāpara–Kali junction, i.e. the time of Kṛṣṇa. That makes it the *present* caturyuga in their own reckoning. This is what lets Bhāskararāya call the 40th, 50th and 60th yugas of the Lakṣmī Tantra "future" (भाविन एव).
- **Madhu–Kaiṭabha "at the end of a kalpa"** precedes the present day of Brahmā altogether, if "kalpānta" is read as the end of the previous kalpa. Neither the text nor the commentaries I read state which kalpa.

## 3. Archaeological and art-historical evidence for the deities  [ACADEMIC]

Main source: **Chiara Policardi, "The Goddess on the Lion: Animal Symbolism in the Representations of the Female Warrior Deity in Kuṣāṇa and Early Gupta India"**, *Cracow Indological Studies* 26.2 (2024), pp. 175–204, <https://doi.org/10.12797/CIS.26.2024.02.07>. This is a peer-reviewed journal article. I downloaded the PDF from <https://journals.akademicka.pl/cis/article/download/5907/5486> and checked every quote below in its text layer.

**There is no name or myth for the earliest images.**
- Abstract: "This figure cannot be identified as Mahiṣāsuramardinī or Durgā in any Pre-Kuṣāṇa and Kuṣāṇa text or inscription."
- p. 183: "Srinivasan (1997: 282) proposes calling her the Warrior Goddess, avoiding the usual epithet Mahiṣāsuramardinī since 'there are no records, to date, which name the Kuṣāṇa Goddess.' In other words, for this goddess there is neither an attested name nor any mythology in the available pre-Gupta Indian sources."

**Udayagiri dates.**
- Policardi, p. 182: "the early Gupta type of Mahiṣāsuramardinī is best preserved in four reliefs at Udayagiri".
- The **401 CE (Gupta year 82) inscription of Candragupta II** at Udayagiri Cave 6 is reported by the sub-agent **from Wikipedia only** ("Udayagiri Caves"). I did not verify it in an epigraphic publication (**UNVERIFIED** here).

**Iconographic development.** Policardi (p. 180), via Srinivasan: in the Kuṣāṇa type the goddess "overcomes the buffalo by breaking his spine and strangulating him with her main hands which hold no weapons". The lion becomes active in the fight only in later, medieval images (Mitterwallner, quoted on p. 182).

**Later monuments**, reported by the sub-agent from Wikipedia only and **not verified by me**: the Mamallapuram relief of Durgā slaying Mahiṣa ("around 630–674 CE"), and 6th-century Maukhari inscriptions at the Nāgārjunī hill cave mentioning her victory over Mahiṣa.

**Relation to the DM text.**
- **The Skandapurāṇa as a source.** Policardi p. 185: the early Skandapurāṇa, "probably composed in North India around the 6th century … (with the oldest manuscript dated to 810/11 CE …)", narrates Vindhyavāsinī's fight with Sumbha and Nisumbha (SkP 62–66). This "probably informed the account of the same feats in the Devīmāhātmya (DM 5–11)".
- **An 8th-century date for the DM.** p. 187: "This text was previously ascribed to between the 5th and 7th centuries, following the dating proposed by Thomas Coburn … (1984). However, in light of other and different typologies of sources, scholars have demonstrated that the composition of this work should be ascribed to the 8th century (Yokochi 2004: 21–23, n. 42)".
  - This is a **later and different dating** from the 5th–6th century consensus in §2.
  - I have it **secondhand through Policardi**. Yokochi's 2004/2005 Groningen thesis (*The rise of the warrior goddess in ancient India*) was not opened. The sub-agent found only its graduation notice, which says the concept of the Supreme Goddess "evolved between the fourth and eighth centuries AD".
  - Whether the 8th-century date is compatible with the Bhandarkar inscription reading (608 CE) is not discussed in what I read. Mirashi's 813 CE reading would fit it.
- **What this means for the episodes.** Images of a goddess subduing a buffalo **predate the DM text by several centuries** (Kuṣāṇa period, about 1st–3rd c. CE). So the Mahiṣa myth, or at least its iconography, is older than the Devī-Māhātmya's version. But in the article's own words, those earliest images have "neither an attested name nor any mythology". **Archaeology dates the cult images, not any event.**
- **For the other episodes** (Madhu–Kaiṭabha, Caṇḍa–Muṇḍa, Raktabīja, Śumbha–Niśumbha), I found no early dated images in the sources I read. The only early textual parallel I found is the Skandapurāṇa's Śumbha–Niśumbha story (via Policardi).

**Other early material, not verified by me.** Kuṣāṇa-period Mātṛkā images ("During the Kushana period (1st century CE to 3rd century CE), the sculptural images of the Matrikas first appear in stone", Wikipedia "Matrikas", per the sub-agent). A popular essay on esamskriti.com (Monidipa Bose Dey; a non-academic web article) reports that the Mathura museum holds six Kuṣāṇa Mahiṣāsuramardinī statuettes, citing V. S. Agrawala, *Artibus Asiae* 21.2 (1958). Neither was checked against a primary or peer-reviewed source.

### 3.1 Lakṣmī Tantra: a textual parallel to the DM 11 prophecies

The commentators (§1.3) take the 40th / 50th / 60th yugas from the Lakṣmī Tantra. I checked the Sanskrit in V. Krishnamacharya's Adyar edition (1959), OCR at <https://archive.org/download/in.ernet.dli.2015.326285/2015.326285.Lakshmi-Tantra_djvu.txt>. The OCR is noisy; the readings below are as printed in the OCR. **Lakṣmī Tantra ch. 9:**

- **The editor's own footnote** (same page) refers these episodes back to the DM: "सुनन्दा-रक्तदन्तिका-शताक्षी-शाकंभरी-दुर्गा-भीमा-भ्रामरीणामुपाख्यानानि मार्कण्डेयपुराणे देवीमाहात्म्ये एकादशाध्याये द्रष्टव्यानि".
- **Translation.** Sanjukta Gupta's English translation was read by the sub-agent and not by me. It renders 9.33: "O Śakra, during that period in the fortieth era, drought and scarcity of water will prevail all over the earth for one hundred years."
**Manvantaras for the main episodes.** The Lakṣmī Tantra gives these too, which the DM does not. From the same OCR (LT ch. 9, नवमोऽध्यायः):
- **LT 9.8**: "महालक्ष्मीरहं शक्र पुनः स्वायंभुवेऽन्तरे । हिताय सर्वलोकानां जाता महिषमर्दिनी". **Mahiṣa** is slain in the **Svāyambhuva (1st) manvantara**. The editor's introduction quotes the same verse, and notes that Bhāskararāya cites it in his commentary on the Lalitā-sahasranāma.
- **LT 9.15–16**: Mahākālī as Yoganidrā kills Madhu and Kaiṭabha. No manvantara is given; the editor's note refers this to DM Ch. 1.
- **LT 9.20–21**: "तामसे त्वन्तरे शक्र महाविद्या हि या परा । गौरीदेहात्समुद्भूता कौशिकीति तदा ह्यहम् ॥ वधाय दुष्टदैत्यानां तथा शुम्भनिशुम्भयोः". **Śumbha–Niśumbha** are slain by Kauśikī in the **Tāmasa (4th) manvantara**.
- **LT 9.28**: "वैवस्वतेऽन्तरे चैतौ पुनः शुम्भनिशुम्भकौ । उत्पत्स्येते …". The second pair comes in Vaivasvata, as in DM 11.41.

**A tension with the DM's frame.** In the DM, Medhas narrates the Śumbha–Niśumbha war to Suratha as past ("purā", GP 5.2) during the **Svārociṣa (2nd)** manvantara (GP 1.4). The Lakṣmī Tantra puts that war in the **Tāmasa (4th)** manvantara, which comes *after* Svārociṣa in the commentators' list (§1.1). I did not find a commentator in DSS7 who discusses this discrepancy. It shows that the manvantara placements are **not consistent across texts**.

**Translation.** Sanjukta Gupta's English translation (OCR <https://archive.org/download/laksmitantratranslationbysanjuktaguptareprintdelhi2003lakshmitantra/LAKSMI-TANTRA-translation-by-Sanjukta-Gupta-reprint-Delhi-2003-LAKSHMI-TANTRA_djvu.txt>) renders LT 9.8–11 as "during the reign of Svayambhuva (Manu) … I, Mahalaksmi, appeared as Mahisamardini". It renders 9.20–21 as "during the period of Tamasa (Manu) I, supreme Mahavidya, was Kausiki". In her notes she refers LT 9 to "Ma.P." chapters 73–76.

**Conclusion.** The Lakṣmī Tantra **adds manvantara and yuga numbers** to episodes the DM leaves undated or dates only as "again / once more". Its prophecy verses match DM 11 nearly verbatim, and its own editor refers them to DM 11. So it is best read as **dependent on the DM**, and its numbers as a later systematisation, not independent evidence. I did not check the Lakṣmī Tantra's own date in this session (**UNVERIFIED**).

## 4. Astronomical or astrological dating attempts

- I found **no published, citable attempt** to date Madhu–Kaiṭabha, Mahiṣāsura, Caṇḍa–Muṇḍa, Raktabīja or Śumbha–Niśumbha astronomically (nakṣatra or planetary positions) or by converting the manvantara/yuga statements into a calendar date. The sub-agent ran several web searches and reported NOT FOUND. I have no source to the contrary.
- The text itself contains **no astronomical data** (planetary positions, nakṣatras, eclipses) for any of these episodes, as far as the verses quoted above and the passages I read show. The only time markers are cosmic units (kalpānta, manvantara, the 28th yuga) and durations (100 years of war; 5,000 years of fighting; a 100-year drought).
- **Related things that are not dating of the events:**
  - Hazra uses the *order* of nakṣatras in another part of the Purāṇa to date that part of the text.
  - The Bengali *akāla-bodhana* (Rāma's out-of-season worship of Durgā) is a later legend not found in the DM. The sub-agent only saw search snippets on it, so this is **UNVERIFIED** here.
- **Conclusion.** Within the sources I could find, nothing dates the mythological events historically or astronomically. Any calendar date offered for them would be a devotional or speculative construction. If one appears in a citable source, it should be reported with its method and its (lack of) scholarly reception. I found none.
